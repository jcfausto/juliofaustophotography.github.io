My goal was simple: go to a selected location and take only three photographs, following a plan I'd prepared in advance.

The result? I came home with 61 photographs instead of the originally planned three!

Why is it so difficult to take just a few carefully planned photographs, while taking dozens feels so much easier and more enjoyable?

That's the question I'd like to explore in this article.

# The Exercise

As part of my journey into architectural photography, I've been setting myself a series of planned and controlled exercises to improve my skills in this genre of photography.

One of them seemed particularly simple: return to a location I'd photographed before and take just three photographs, each with a specific purpose and composition planned in advance, so that I didn't need to do much thinking on location.

Three photographs. How difficult could that be?

# The Setting

To make things even easier, I decided to bring only one camera, one lens, and a tripod. Just the basic equipment necessary for the exercise. I also decided to instead of using my regular photography backpack, to use a smaller shoulder back to carry the equipment. More practical and easier to use than the large backpack.

The location was the _Haus der Kulturen der Welt_ in Berlin, specifically its southern elevation, around sunset and dusk.

I'd even planned the camera settings I'd use for most of the photographs:

- Aperture Priority (A)
- ISO 200
- f/5.6

Everything was planned. All I had to do was show up and execute.

Or so I thought.

# The Goals

The exercise had three specific goals:

1. **Photograph the building within its landscape**

The first photograph should show the building in its surroundings and its relationship with the landscape.

This is often an important image in architectural photography, as it helps establish the building's context and reveals how it relates to the surrounding urban environment.

2. **Show human scale and movement**

The second photograph should communicate the monumental scale of the building.

By including people in carefully chosen positions within the composition, I wanted to emphasise the building's proportions and its relationship with the people who experience it.

3. **Capture "The Wings"**

The third photograph should focus on the building's distinctive roof, whose sweeping shape resembles a pair of wings.

My intention was to capture not only the architectural form but also the symbolism behind it: the idea of freedom expressed through the building's design.

Three photographs, three clear objectives. What could possibly go wrong?

# The Shooting

When I arrived, the sun was only a few minutes away from setting. I had a little time to find a suitable position for the first photograph. I walked toward a spot I'd identified during my earlier scouting visit, but it wasn't quite right. At this time of year, too many trees were obstructing the view of the building.

So I walked around a little more, looking for another position. Eventually, I found a spot that seemed promising and took a few shots. And that's where the problem began.

Did you notice how I said a few shots?

I was supposed to take just one photograph of this composition. The whole point of the exercise was to slow down, observe carefully, and make deliberate compositional decisions instead of taking dozens of photographs in the hope that one of them would turn out well.

But almost immediately, I fell back into my usual mode: photograph first, figure it out later.

At the time, I didn't really notice what was happening. Looking back, however, I realize that I was anxious about finding a composition that would satisfy the exercise while also worrying about the rapidly fading light.

The sun wasn't going to wait for me. And then things went completely off script.

After that first composition, I was seduced by the colours appearing in the sky. Beautiful shades of orange and purple were developing behind the building, and I couldn't resist taking a few more photographs.

Then the reflections in the pond caught my attention. Like a mosquito drawn to a light source, I found myself moving toward the water, excited by the changing light and reflections.

![](/assets/images/photography/journal/three-photos-sixty-one-attempts/20261016-_A160671.jpg)

Naturally, I took a few more shots. Before I knew it, blue hour had arrived, the sun was gone, the sky was turning deep blue, and I was still there with my camera and tripod.

![](/assets/images/photography/journal/three-photos-sixty-one-attempts/20261016-_A160690.jpg)

Well, since I was already there, why not take a few dusk photographs? A few photographs later, I came back home with 61 photographs!

Not three. Sixty-one.

# Conclusion and Lessons Learned

So, did I fail the exercise?

Technically, yes. If this had been a graded assignment, I would have completely flunked it.

But I'm not particularly upset about it. I actually had a great time, and I came home with several photographs that I genuinely enjoy.

![](/assets/images/photography/journal/three-photos-sixty-one-attempts/20261016-_A160691.jpg)

Still, the experience made me realize something important.

Taking photographs is easy. Taking photographs deliberately, with a clear intention and the discipline to stick to it, is much harder.

I think exercises like this one can be incredibly valuable for developing my skills. They force me to slow down, pay closer attention to composition, and become more conscious of the decisions I make before pressing the shutter.

They might also help me become more comfortable with the anxiety I sometimes experience while photographing, especially when I feel pressured to find the right composition before the light disappears.

But I learned something else, too.

**Photography is supposed to be enjoyable.**

And despite completely abandoning my original plan, I had fun. Perhaps that's just as important.

After all, I can return to the same location a few more times to complete the photographs for my architectural essay. There's no deadline, no client waiting for the results, and no reason to rush.

In the meantime, I can keep experimenting, making mistakes, and learning from the experience.

Finally, this exercise gave me an even greater appreciation for photographers who have developed the ability to create remarkable images with just a handful of carefully considered compositions.

There's a lot more observation, knowledge, patience, and experience behind that ability than we might initially imagine.

Next time, I'll try to stick to the rules.

But no promises.
